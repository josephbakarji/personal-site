# What Are We Conserving?

I try to think, brainstorm out loud while walking, write, and rewrite every morning. This morning, after having recorded forty minutes of thinking out loud about what it has been like to spend the past year doing most of my work inside a language model, I received an email from my dean, forwarding MIT President Kornbluth's message and the [final report](https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/) of MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training. He recommended reading it as we prepare our Fall courses.

So let me start with being transparent about where I am (mentally) before I say anything about the report. For the past year I have gone all in on using LLMs for my work for a couple of reasons. First, I couldn't help but be curious about their capabilities and whether they can solve all the complex problems and discuss all the gnarly questions I've had brewing for years. Second, my bottleneck has always been my own time and attention, and I wanted to see if I could use these systems to get more done in the same amount of time. Overall, I must say, I have been able to explore more ideas, write more, and spend more time thinking and formulating problems than I ever could have on my own. 

BUT (yes there's always one),

there's a cost, and I'm not sure I'm ready to say that the tradeoff is worth it. I have been thinking about this a lot, and I have been trying to figure out what exactly is being lost in this new way of working.

What I can say is that most of my work now happens through conversations with a machine. This is not a thought experiment for me and it is not a policy question I get to consider from a safe distance. I am, unfortunately or not, a case study. Whatever I say next about students should be read as something I am also saying about myself.

## The Report Is Right About the Symptoms

I enjoyed reading the report. The MIT committee did some serious work, unsurprisingly, and the parts of it that will be quoted are the parts that deserve to be. Attendance at office hours is down. Online discussions are insanely hard to get going (I tried many things). It makes sense that study groups have decreased. The report claims the reason is: education is collapsing into a transaction where "assignments are outputs, teachers are evaluators, peers are optional." It calls the thing that happens when a student receives a correct answer without having struggled for it *cognitive surrender*, which is a pretty good phrase to represent the emotional state many people (not only students) find themselves in these days.

The report also has numbers, which is what gives it teeth. Ninety-three percent of MIT undergraduates do research through UROP: the Undergraduate Research Opportunities Program. In the Fall 2025 survey, two thirds of students said AI would be important to their careers, but only a quarter felt MIT had prepared them for it. That gap is most of the problem stated as a statistic.

And the recommendations mostly make sense. Redesign assessment around oral exams, portfolios, and conversation. Push hands-on and experiential work. Build structured in-person collaboration into every subject rather than hoping it happens. Refuse AI detection software, because it does not work and because using it tells students you have decided to police them rather than teach them. Write an explicit policy for every course and state the reason behind it, not just the rule. I agree with almost all of this, and I'll try to change my courses accordingly.

But I think the report is diagnosing at the wrong scale, and I want to say why, because the scale you diagnose at determines what you think you are protecting.

## The Unit Is Moving

The report's unit of analysis is the individual student acquiring mastery. Everything it recommends is a conservation measure for that unit. Protect the struggle. Protect the confidence. Protect the social contract between instructor and student. It is a defense of a structure whose center is one person becoming capable.

What I've been thinking about this morning is focused at a slightly bigger scale. Intelligence, in its very nature, is an act of coarse graining, of going from one scale to the next. A bacterium is intelligent: it senses, it navigates, it persists. The cells in your body are intelligent in the same way, except that they self-organized into something that can act at a scale no cell can reach. A cell operates at micrometers. You operate at meters. That transition is not an improvement in cellular intelligence. It is a change in what scale of the world is available to the cell for it to act on at all.

We have been doing this for a long time. Societies are already larger-scale organisms. They encode structure that no individual holds: law, currency, institutions, markets, and the whole apparatus by which a million people coordinate without meeting. I want to be careful here, because there is a tempting overstatement. It is not that language models gave the social scale a language; it already had several. What they gave it is a conversational interface. The social organism can now answer back, in real time at the speed of thought. You can talk to a medical field and a legal system that can reason at scales the average person cannot. That is new, and I think framing it in terms of scale is the actual event.

What follows from a scale transition is fairly predictable, because we have an analogy with the biology. Behavioral regulation migrates upward in scales. The lower scale keeps its local business and outsources coordination. Your cells still handle their own metabolism; they do not consult the brain about ATP. But increasingly, as they become more nested into higher scale intelligent systems, their behavior in relation to each other is governed by something bigger than them.

This is a bit uncomfortable to think about. Individuals keep their local lives and outsource judgment. Not skill, not knowledge, and not, at first, anything anyone will miss. Judgment. The question of what is worth doing next.

## The Conservation Law the Report Is Missing

Here I'll try to push the analogy even further.

A body works because its cells remain locally viable. A cell does not need the brain in order to survive; it needs the brain in order to coordinate with the rest, so that the larger system it belongs to can survive. If you built an organism whose cells could not metabolize without a signal from the center, you would not have built a robust organism; you would have built something that dies the moment the signal drops, and that has no capacity to reorganize when the center is wrong.

So the failure mode is not that students use AI. The failure mode is students who cannot metabolize without the hub.

That reframes the recommendations the MIT report is making. An oral exam is not an anti-cheating device. It is a local viability test. You are not checking whether a student wrote their own code; you are checking whether anything in them can still run when the connection is cut. Hands-on lab work is not nostalgia for a slower era. It is the part of the curriculum where the immediate world around, tactile and tangible with real-world physical stakes, rather than a model, is the thing that tells you that you are wrong. In-person collaboration is not a social nicety from another era that we can now just drop. COVID taught us that much. It is the peer-to-peer coupling that lets the lower scale coordinate without routing everything through a hub. 

Written that way, the syllabus question is not "may you use AI in this course." It is: what has to remain locally viable in you at the end of this semester? The next question should be: how would we know?

## The Discriminator Problem

Now let me turn this on myself, because it is the reason I don't think the report is just being conservative.

The strategy that has made this year productive for me works like stochastic gradient descent. I have far more ideas than I have time, and the more creative an idea is, the lower its probability of working out. Historically, that meant choosing one, spending months on it, and often being wrong; because I aimed too high. The obvious correction is to be more conservative, to pick ideas that make incremental progress on questions I've already explored. I do some of that, and a fair criticism of my approach is that I should do more of it. 

But now I can spend my time thinking about the idea, formulating the question, handing it to a machine, and letting it simulate where the idea goes. Instead of trying one thing a day I try a hundred: a low quality ensemble approach. It doesn't matter if most of them are wrong or shallow. The assumption is that many cheap noisy trials beat one slow accurate one. The same reason an ensemble of weak models beats a single accurate but brittle one.

But this ensemble approach would only work if *evaluation* (i.e. judgement) is cheap and correct. I can look at a hundred results and know in ten seconds which one is promising. That ten seconds is the load-bearing point that puts me in the loop of development. And it was paid for over more than a decade of deriving things by hand, making errors, correcting them, and slowly acquiring a feel for what a real result looks like without having to look at the code.

My students do not have that yet. Which means the exact strategy that is making me more productive would, applied to them at this stage, make them hollow; the worry the MIT report highlights. That's not because struggle is morally better. It's just that struggle is the process by which a discriminator gets calibrated, and without a discriminator, scale amplifies noise. And this is what I experience when I run 10 projects in parallel: my cognitive load shifts from constructively building a solution from building blocks I'm confident using, to judging whether what something else has built for me is misleading or wrong. 

So I do not think the concern is that students are avoiding difficulty. I think the concern is that we are handing them a generator before they have an evaluator, and the generator is good enough to make the absence of an evaluator invisible for several years until reality with all its complexity hits them in the face. I must say that this was partially a problem anyway with educational systems before LLMs. Quizzes and exams also masked the complexity of the real world and transferred judgement to the teacher who often had no more information than the student. But the scale of the problem is now larger, and the consequences are more severe.

## Where I Part Ways

Of the report's eight principles, seven I would sign. The one I would rewrite is "augmentation not automation."

It is a tool framing, and this is not a tool. A hammer lets you drive a nail without destroying your hand; it acts on the world so that you do not have to, and it leaves you intact. That is the definition of a tool from the user's side. What is happening between people and these systems is not that. It is a coupling: you shape it and it shapes you, and after enough cycles the boundary is hard to locate. I do not talk the way I did two years ago. I do not decide what to work on the way I did two years ago.

"Augmentation not automation" cannot tell the difference between a student who has been augmented into capability and one who has been augmented into dependence, because both look like augmentation from outside. The honest version of the principle is harder to put on a slide: choose what you couple with, and understand that it will change you. Some couplings leave you larger. Some leave you unable to stand alone. The report's own recommendations are, I think, groping toward exactly this distinction without a vocabulary for it.

## What Nobody in This Conversation Is Talking About

This is where my morning memo went, and I think it is the question underneath the whole report. What is it that makes a good society, a good life, and good mental health?

And research shows that the things that reliably make people well are embarrassingly physical. [Eat well](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12916-017-0791-y). [Move every day](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847), and a surprising amount of pain and low confidence turns out to have been about not moving. [Be in a room with other people](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316); watch what happens to their faces and have deep conversations with their entire physical being. Play an instrument, which is one of the most thoroughly embodied things a person can do to enter a state of flow. Dance. Remember when we ran the controlled experiment on this in 2020, took the physical dimension away while intensifying the informational one, and watched what it did to [everyone's mental health](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2821%2902143-7)?

Almost none of this shows up in how we talk about AI's promise, and I think it has to do with incentives. The public justification for building LLMs at scale, despite the risks, is almost entirely around the minimum: curing disease, ending poverty, relieving suffering. Those are real problems that I think we should certainly tackle. But they describe pulling people up to a floor, and they are nearly silent about the life above the floor, the well-rounded, ordinary but good one, which is what most of the people I know are actually trying to build. And it's one that requires balance rather than extremes.

The same problem shows up in what students are, either implicitly or explicitly conditioned to want. An artist aims at millions of listeners and a scientist at millions of readers. But, while you can't meet a million people to build meaningful relationships with them, relationships at that scale have become technically possible, in some shape or form, so they became the thing worth wanting, and every individual is now incentivized to try to become part of the brain rather than to be well inside their own circle. This is exactly what you would predict from a system reorganizing at a higher scale, and it is exactly what the report means, I think, when it says to put "humanity front and center". But what's the mechanism?

The mechanism, if I had to state it, is that health has to hold across scales simultaneously. A well person is a well society of cells, which is why sleep and food and movement are not lifestyle advice but structural biological facts. A well society is one made of well people. A functioning ecosystem synchronizes those scales in a complex balancing act. Any proposal that improves one scale by degrading the one below it is not a stable, permanent, or scalable solution. It's a temporary transfer of instability.

## The Disillusionment of The Moment 

The report's first principle is "be humble" on the grounds that the technology is moving quickly and we will need course corrections. True, but I'll take it a step further.

I think the best discoveries are disillusionments. You assume something, and you find out you are wrong, and for a brief moment before you rebuild your new set of assumptions, you glimpse something quite deep about yourself. Then you get used to it. You build structures around the new understanding, you make new assumptions about how the world works, and you enter what ultimately becomes the next illusion: an overconfidence in a world model you comfortably sit in without questioning. The interval between the two is the only time most of us see clearly.

These few years are such an interval that will ultimately pass. We will settle on policies and platforms and best practices, and within a few years we will have forgotten that we invented them, and they will be as invisible to us as the ones this report is currently dismantling. So the thing worth teaching is not the policy. It is how to stand in the interval without grabbing for the nearest solid structure: permanent curiosity, a working knowledge that every assumption you hold will eventually crumble, and a sharp awareness of the paradox that "all models are wrong but some are useful" (George Box), which is a statement about how to live and not only about statistics.

This is frightening, and it is only frightening in proportion to what we are holding onto. It is also the most interesting moment to be a scientist or a teacher that I expect to see, because we are being forced to re-derive things we thought were settled. I am excited and scared, roughly equally, accepting that living in the comfort of knowing was always the more illusory way to live. We are conserving something in all of this. The question I keep coming back to is what, exactly, and at which scale it lives.
